# B2B LinkedIn Organic Growth Playbook (SOP)

**Built from:** `research/` - 10 practitioners, 49 collected LinkedIn posts, added 17–19 June 2026.
**Chosen topic:** How a B2B founder/operator builds a LinkedIn presence that compounds, using organic content only (no paid distribution - that was out of scope for the underlying research by design).

> **Before you read this:** this is a complete first draft, built from the research already in this repo plus a handful of independent checks I ran on specific claims (flagged inline as "verified independently"). The four "where experts disagree" calls, the three rejections, and the "who I wouldn't recommend" section are *my* reasoning applied to the evidence - not a neutral consensus. The brief for this exercise evaluates independent judgment, so treat these as a strong starting position to argue with, not a finished verdict. Swap in your own view anywhere you land differently.

## The SOP

### Step 1 - Fix positioning before you touch content
Before you optimize *what* you post, fix your headline, banner, and featured section. Inconsistent posting that isn't converting is frequently a positioning problem wearing a content problem's clothes - people who show up daily and still get nothing often haven't told visitors who they help or why to care in the 3 seconds before they scroll on (source: Lara Acostar, 12.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_i-built-this-to-share-with-my-private-clients-activity-7470418239987212288-qGa7)).

### Step 2 - Earn attention with story before authority
Lead with where you came from and what you've actually built before you lead with what you know - audiences generally don't extend credit for expertise until they've been shown the track record behind it (source: Matt Barker, 11.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_you-can-be-worth-40m-but-still-get-ignored-activity-7468632307122421760-T_tL)). Structurally, this means opening posts with a concrete, specific story in the first few lines, stating the lesson explicitly, backing it with scannable proof points, and closing with a direct question to the reader (source: Lara Acostar, 12.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_theres-100s-of-ways-to-get-more-followers-activity-7469695786713473025-XxGK)).

*(This is a default, not a law - see [Disagreement 3](#where-experts-disagree) for when to skip the story and open with data instead.)*

### Step 3 - Build your AI context files before you prompt anything
If AI is part of your content workflow, build short, standing context files - who you are, your voice, your non-negotiable rules - that get read automatically before every task, instead of re-explaining yourself in every prompt. Keep them lean: a bloated context file gets re-read (and paid for, in tokens and in quality) on every single task (source: Ruben Hassid, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ruben-hassid_delete-almost-every-prompt-you-ever-saved-activity-7472603969618337793-jn2X)). At a system level, the same idea scales into four layers - context, skills, orchestration, integrations - so a new hire or freelancer can be handed a working setup instead of starting from zero (source: Kyle Poyar, 18.06.2026, citing GTM consultant Matteo Tittarelli's framework - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kyle-poyar_a-4-step-guide-to-build-your-ai-gtm-system-activity-7473001162556964865-qJ8o)).

### Step 4 - Pick a default post format, and know your exceptions
Default to short posts (under ~100 words) anchored by a single link. This isn't a stylistic preference - it's the conclusion of an actual controlled test: one practitioner ran a 30-day experiment restricting himself to sub-100-word posts, at least one link, and zero carousels/infographics, built specifically to test whether short posts drive more link clicks than heavier formats (source: Matt Barker, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_i-did-it-i-completed-my-30-day-linkedin-activity-7472618597664813056-4--S)).

Break from that default for genuinely reference-worthy material - frameworks, checklists, anything worth saving - where a dense, infographic-paired post earns its heavier production cost (source: Eric Partaker, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ericpartaker_a-full-operations-book-in-one-page-heres-share-7472906868839911425-SH1P)). *(See [Disagreement 2](#where-experts-disagree) for the full argument on which should be your default.)*

Also break from it when you get first-hand access most of your audience can't get - a conference, an embargoed briefing, a founder conversation. Post the specific, concrete things you learned within a few days, not just proof you were in the room (source: Ishan Sharma, 14.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ishansharma7390_business-startup-meta-activity-7471548508517539840-hSIf)).

### Step 5 - Earn credibility with sourced data, and be honest about what's confirmed vs. inferred
Once a week or so, publish something built on real data - and when you build on someone else's research, name them in the text, not just in a buried link. One source in this research does this consistently: crediting Christoph Janz's "hunting animals" SaaS framework by name rather than presenting the underlying idea as original (source: Kyle Poyar, 04.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kyle-poyar_the-initial-decision-about-which-customer-activity-7467928850438627330-guvB)). I checked this one independently: Janz's framework is real, originated in a 2014 Point Nine Capital post, and is still cited across the SaaS/VC world today (verified independently - [medium.com/point-nine-news](https://medium.com/point-nine-news/five-years-later-five-ways-to-build-a-100-million-saas-business-49ebca14825c)). That's the citation discipline worth copying.

When you comment on breaking industry news, do it within 24–48 hours and structure the take as short, arrow-bulleted "what this means" points rather than raw reaction (source: Jess Ramos, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jessramosmsba_microsoft-partnering-with-openclaw-was-not-activity-7472690066277191680-ILc7)). One caution worth building into this habit permanently: two sources in this research (Jess Ramos, 15.06.2026; Aaron Levie, 14.06.2026) covered the same live story - the Claude Fable 5/Mythos 5 export-control suspension - and both offered a specific causal explanation (that the models could be manipulated to expose software vulnerabilities) days before Anthropic's own account confirmed the mechanism. They turned out to be substantially right (verified independently - Anthropic, "Redeploying Claude Fable 5," 30.06.2026, [anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5](https://www.anthropic.com/news/redeploying-fable-5)), but at the time of posting, that specificity was an inference presented with the confidence of a confirmed fact. The habit to build: mark your own speculation as speculation in the post itself, even when you turn out to be right.

### Step 6 - Match the post's shape to the idea, not to a template
Not every idea needs a 10-bullet framework. A single sharp, contrarian observation in 2–5 lines can outperform an over-structured post when the whole point is the punchline (source: Jason Lemkin, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jasonmlemkin_the-pizza-parlor-picks-up-the-phone-for-a-activity-7473020932102742016-bCFe)). For longer, thought-leadership-style posts on genuinely unsettled questions, use plain sub-bulleted structure and explicitly flag your own opinion as opinion when the topic is contested - don't blur your take into the facts (source: Aaron Levie, 14.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/boxaaron_the-big-news-last-night-was-that-the-us-government-activity-7471588067246747648-bDv0)). For posts explaining an abstract concept, open with a small, concrete, relatable scene - even a personal or family anecdote - before naming the underlying idea; it's a lower-lift hook than a dramatic personal-struggle story and still earns attention (source: Saheli Chatterjee, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/saheli-chatterjee-organic-marketing_for-a-month-i-watched-my-sister-obsess-over-activity-7472864729519001601-Zt4o)).

### Step 7 - Decide, in advance, where AI is and isn't allowed to touch the words
Use AI to interview you and extract a real memory or story through structured questioning - but don't let it draft the finished post. Treat it as an extraction and editing tool for your voice, not a ghostwriter for it (source: Matt Barker, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_i-built-a-claude-skill-that-finds-the-story-activity-7472980970242109440-YAoe)). *(This is the more defensible half of a real disagreement in the research - see [Disagreement 4](#where-experts-disagree).)*

### Step 8 - Keep the actual payoff close to the hook
When you promise a resource, put it directly in the post or one click away. Every extra step between the hook and the value (subscribe → find an email → click a specific button → find a folder) is a place someone drops off before getting what you promised them. *(I'm stating this as the adopted rule; the full argument against heavier gating, and the case for it, is in [Disagreement 1](#where-experts-disagree) and [Rejected idea 1](#what-i-rejected-and-why).)*

### Step 9 - Run a weekly review loop, not just a weekly posting schedule
Review your own post performance on a fixed cadence, and treat your account as a running experiment: form a hypothesis before you post, not just a story about the results after. One source in this research ran exactly this kind of test - a defined, time-boxed change with a real before/after comparison - rather than posting on instinct and rationalizing the results afterward (source: Matt Barker, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_i-did-it-i-completed-my-30-day-linkedin-activity-7472618597664813056-4--S)). *(I take this further in [My original ideas](#my-original-ideas) below.)*

## Where experts disagree

### 1. Should the free resource be handed over directly, or gated behind a multi-step funnel?

**What Ruben Hassid recommends:** gate it. A representative post walks readers through a 9-step process to get one free "skill" file - go to his site, subscribe, wait for a welcome email, find the reply button inside it, open a linked Notion page, navigate to a specific folder, download. Several of his other posts repeat close variations of the same subscribe → email → Notion pattern (source: Ruben Hassid, 15.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ruben-hassid_how-to-never-be-stuck-on-i-dont-know-how-activity-7472513317341884416-520Q)).

**What Saheli Chatterjee recommends:** give it directly. Her post listing 10 free AI/Claude learning resources puts working links right in the post itself - no email gate, no multi-step unlock (source: Saheli Chatterjee, 14.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/saheli-chatterjee-organic-marketing_claude-aiformarketers-learnai-activity-7471442923310534656-BN0F)).

**Which side I take, and why:** Saheli's, as the default. A B2B reader's time is the scarce resource in this exchange, and every added unlock step is a place to lose them before they get the thing that made them stop scrolling. Multi-step gates mostly serve the poster's list-building goal, not the stated audience goal of "solve this now." That said, I don't think Ruben's approach is simply wrong - an email list is a real, durable, owned asset that a link-in-post never builds, and that trade-off is legitimate. My rule: gate genuinely substantial, effort-intensive assets (a full toolkit, a template library) where the friction is proportionate to the value; don't gate a link list.

### 2. Should your default post be short and link-anchored, or long and infographic-dense?

**What Matt Barker recommends:** short. His own 30-day, no-carousel experiment was built specifically to test whether ≤100-word posts with a link outperform heavier formats for driving link clicks - the metric he cares about (source: Matt Barker, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_i-did-it-i-completed-my-30-day-linkedin-activity-7472618597664813056-4--S)).

**What Eric Partaker recommends:** long and visual. His consistent pattern is dense, multi-framework posts paired with a downloadable, high-resolution infographic (source: Eric Partaker, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ericpartaker_a-full-operations-book-in-one-page-heres-share-7472906868839911425-SH1P)).

**Which side I take, and why:** it genuinely depends on the metric you're optimizing, but if I have to set a default for a B2B founder starting from zero, I'd pick Barker's. It's far cheaper to produce consistently, it directly targets the off-platform action (a link click) that most early B2B funnels actually need, and Partaker's format assumes either design skill or budget most solo founders don't have on day one. This isn't "never do infographics" - once you have something genuinely save-worthy (a proprietary framework, a checklist), the heavier format earns its cost. It's "don't default to it before you've earned that."

### 3. Should you open with a personal story, or open with data?

**What Lara Acostar and Matt Barker recommend:** story first, always. Lara's framework opens every post with three lines of story before the lesson; Matt states plainly that people won't care what you know until you've shown where you came from and what you've built (source: Lara Acostar, 12.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_theres-100s-of-ways-to-get-more-followers-activity-7469695786713473025-XxGK); Matt Barker, 11.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_you-can-be-worth-40m-but-still-get-ignored-activity-7468632307122421760-T_tL)).

**What Kyle Poyar recommends:** skip the story, open with the number. His posts routinely open cold with hard data - "Data from 1,043 companies: 70% of them still target the same customer..." - with zero narrative framing (source: Kyle Poyar, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kyle-poyar_data-from-1043-companies-70-of-them-still-activity-7472629768253968386-lsZk)).

**Which side I take, and why:** this isn't really a disagreement about what works - it's a disagreement that dissolves once you account for starting authority. Poyar can open cold with data because his track record (15 years in SaaS growth, ex-OpenView Operating Partner, the newsletter's own 85,000+ subscriber base - verified independently) already carries credibility before the sentence starts. A founder posting into a cold or small audience doesn't have that yet; for them, a story is the only credibility mechanism available until they've generated proprietary data of their own. My rule: default to story-first until you have your own numbers worth leading with, then graduate to data-first. It's a sequence, not a permanent camp.

### 4. Should AI write the post, or only ever assist around the edges?

**What Ruben Hassid recommends:** let AI do the heavy production lift directly - his content walks readers through feeding Claude a long "mega-prompt," applying a pre-built design file, and generally producing finished output at volume (source: Ruben Hassid, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ruben-hassid_vibecoding-activity-7472875732130689024-y--k)).

**What Matt Barker recommends:** never let it write the actual post. He built a tool specifically so AI interviews the user and hands back structured notes - and stops there - arguing that's exactly why "everyone sounds the same now" when every other tool happily finishes the post for you (source: Matt Barker, 17.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_i-built-a-claude-skill-that-finds-the-story-activity-7472980970242109440-YAoe)).

**Which side I take, and why:** I split this by job type rather than picking one wholesale. Ruben's approach is right for production work that isn't the "voice" of the post - design systems, research, editing, repurposing. Barker's caution is right for the specific words a reader will read as your take. The rule in [Step 7](#the-sop): AI can extract, structure, and edit; a human writes the opening lines and the actual point. I lean toward protecting Barker's rule more strictly, because a small account's voice is the one thing a competitor can't copy - losing it to generic AI phrasing is a bigger, harder-to-reverse risk than losing a few hours of production speed.

## What I rejected and why

**1. Manufactured urgency and scarcity mechanics.** Multiple posts from one source use repeating countdown deadlines ("taking the link down in 42hrs," then reopening the same offer "for the next 24 hours" days later), an application-only gate, a password readers must DM to get a reply, and a dramatized "I'm leaking my phone number and I'm kinda scared" hook (source: Lara Acostar, 17.06.2026 - [link 1](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_im-leaking-my-phone-number-and-im-kinda-activity-7472971360458342400-E4c-); 12.06.2026 - [link 2](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/laraacostar_i-built-this-to-share-with-my-private-clients-activity-7470418239987212288-qGa7)).

I'm not putting this in the SOP, for two reasons. First, it's a borrowed consumer-marketing mechanic, and B2B buyers on longer, more considered purchase cycles tend to be more skeptical of urgency theater than consumer audiences - and reusing the same "42 hours left" framing across multiple posts undercuts it further; if the deadline keeps reappearing, it wasn't really a deadline. Second, unlike Matt Barker's link-click experiment, nothing in this research shows before/after data that this specific tactic actually outperforms a plain, always-available link - recommending it would mean asking you to trust an unverified claim, which fails the source-skepticism bar I'm trying to hold the rest of this document to.

**2. Treating sponsored or brand-invited coverage as a repeatable growth technique.** Several posts read as detailed, favorable coverage of a company's product or event that the author was invited to or hosted by, with product mentions woven through as if organic observation, and no disclosure of whether the visit or coverage was compensated (source: Ishan Sharma, 14.06.2026, Meta Business Agent - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ishansharma7390_business-startup-meta-activity-7471548508517539840-hSIf); 18.05.2026, JioFinance - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ishansharma7390_i-found-the-super-app-for-finance-activity-7458536210269986816-pUoo)).

I'm rejecting this as an SOP technique, not because it's illegitimate for the poster, but because it's not repeatable for the audience this playbook is written for. Getting invited to launch events and given products to cover is downstream of already having reach - it's a reward for prior growth, not a lever for building reach from zero. It also blurs opinion and brand messaging in a way a reader can't independently evaluate, which is a real credibility cost I don't want to build into a founder's early playbook.

**3. Content-free curiosity bait.** One post announces "I'm building something," explicitly withholds any detail ("I won't tell you what it is yet... any ideas? Drop your guess below"), and pairs the teaser with an affiliate-style discount link to a third-party tool (source: Eric Partaker, 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/ericpartaker_ad-lovablepartner-activity-7472619034174394369-RAI2)).

I'm rejecting this specific pattern, not Eric Partaker's approach generally (his framework-and-infographic format is recommended in Step 4). A pure curiosity gap asks the reader to do work (guessing in the comments) in exchange for zero information in the post itself - a weaker value exchange than his framework posts - and it's paired here with what looks like an affiliate incentive, which is easy to overuse until an audience stops taking the "I'll reveal it soon" bait.

## My original ideas

### 1. The Hypothesis Ledger

**The idea:** a recurring, dated post format - weekly or every two weeks - with a fixed structure: (a) restate the specific hypothesis from your *previous* ledger entry, (b) report the actual result against it, numbers included even when they're small or unflattering, (c) state the next hypothesis you're about to test. Each entry names and links the prior one, so the series reads as one continuing, serialized story rather than disconnected updates.

**Why it isn't already in the sources:** Matt Barker ran one 30-day experiment and revealed the recap as a single result, partly gated behind his paid community (source: 16.06.2026 - [link](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/mattjbarker1_i-did-it-i-completed-my-30-day-linkedin-activity-7472618597664813056-4--S)). Kyle Poyar publishes rigorous data, but it's about other companies' aggregate behavior, not his own account's week-to-week performance. None of the 10 sources runs a standing, numbered, self-referential hypothesis-then-result loop as their recurring content spine.

**Why it could work:**
- It solves the cold-start problem in [Disagreement 3](#where-experts-disagree) - you don't need pre-existing authority or proprietary data to start; the ledger manufactures fresh, honest material every single cycle purely from the act of running the account. It also makes literal exactly what this research's own selection rule already values: "practitioners who practice what they post" (per `README.md`) - instead of claiming that, the ledger makes it auditable.
- It creates a genuine open loop - a reason to come back for entry #7 specifically because you read #6 - the way a serialized story creates anticipation, which a one-off post structurally can't do.
- It removes the temptation toward the manufactured urgency I rejected above, because the post is inherently time-bound and consequential (this cycle's real result) without needing a fake countdown attached.
- Committing to publish the result *whether or not it's good* discourages cherry-picking only wins, which should build a more differentiated, more trusted voice in a research set that's currently crowded with unverifiable, self-reported numbers (see [Weaknesses](#weaknesses-of-this-playbook)).

I haven't run this myself - it's a hypothesis about a format, presented in a document that's otherwise trying to hold everything else to an evidence standard, so I want to be direct that this idea doesn't yet meet that bar itself.

### 2. Source-skepticism as content, not just as process

**The idea:** whenever you cite an external stat or claim - yours or someone else's - add one line showing what you actually checked and what you couldn't confirm ("I checked X against Y; couldn't verify Z independently"). Make the verification itself part of the post, not something that happens invisibly before you hit publish.

**Why it isn't already in the sources:** every practitioner in this research cites (or doesn't cite) claims, but none of them show their verification work in the post itself - readers see the conclusion, not the checking. This document does show that work throughout (e.g., independently confirming Kyle Poyar's subscriber count and Christoph Janz's original framework in Step 5), and I think that transparency is itself a differentiator in a niche where most claims - follower counts, revenue figures, subscriber totals - are unverifiable self-reports from the poster. It's a smaller, more tactical idea than the Hypothesis Ledger, but it's a natural pairing with it: the ledger supplies honest first-party data, this format supplies honest handling of everyone else's.

## Weaknesses of this playbook

- **Tiny, short-window sample.** 10 accounts, each represented by their 4–5 most recent posts as of a 3-day collection window (17–19 June 2026). That week happened to include a live, fast-moving AI regulation story (the Fable/Mythos suspension), which shaped a noticeable share of the collected content. A different week would likely show a different mix of formats.
- **Most "performance" numbers are self-reported, not verified.** Follower counts, subscriber counts, impressions, and revenue figures throughout the research (192,000 followers, 737,000 newsletter readers, $3M+ "driven for clients," 650K followers, 300k+ followers) come from the posters themselves, inside posts that are also selling something. I was able to verify Kyle Poyar's subscriber count and Christoph Janz's framework against sources outside this repo; I could not do the same for the others, because LinkedIn's own follower data isn't accessible without logging in. Treat every other specific number in this playbook as claimed, not confirmed.
- **Heavy "selling something" bias in the sample.** Most of these 10 monetize an audience directly - courses, bootcamps, communities, coaching, a paid newsletter tier. None of that makes their tactics wrong, but it does mean the sample has a live incentive to make organic growth look more effective than it might be for someone with no offer to sell, e.g., an in-house B2B marketer posting from a company page.
- **Some of the best "reactive news" examples won't repeat identically.** The Fable/Mythos coverage worked because it was one specific, live story; the *tactic* (react fast, structure the take, flag inference vs. fact) is durable, but the next cycle's story will need the same discipline applied fresh, not this example copied.
- **LinkedIn-only, organic-only, by the original research's own design** (per `README.md`). This playbook says nothing about paid amplification, cross-posting, or whether any of this transfers to X, YouTube, or newsletters - several of these practitioners clearly run those too, but that material wasn't collected here.
- **Untested by me.** Every recommendation here is an inference from watching 10 other people's public behavior, not a result I've produced on a live account. The Hypothesis Ledger idea in particular is speculative until someone actually runs it for a few cycles.
- **A screened list, not a representative one.** The 10 were deliberately selected as hands-on practitioners, explicitly excluding commentators and aggregators (per `README.md`). That's a defensible choice for this research question, but it means this playbook reflects patterns among individual creator-operators with a personal offer to sell - not, say, in-house B2B marketing teams, which is a meaningfully different context with different constraints (brand approval, legal review, no personal "story" to lead with).

## Who I would NOT recommend following and why

**Lara Acostar - would not recommend, at least not as a primary source for a B2B founder.** Her content concentrates the research's heaviest use of manufactured urgency: repeating countdown deadlines, an application-only gate, a password-to-DM mechanic, and a dramatized "leaking my phone number" hook, all inside a single 3-day window of collected posts (see [Rejected idea 1](#what-i-rejected-and-why)). Her specific performance claims (300k+ followers, "a million-dollar business," "$40M+") appear only inside promotional posts, with nothing in this research I could check them against independently, unlike Kyle Poyar's newsletter numbers or Christoph Janz's framework. Her SLAY framework itself is a reasonable, teachable structure (story → lesson → listicle → question) - but that same structure is visible, unbranded, in both Saheli Chatterjee's and Matt Barker's posts, so following her specifically doesn't teach anything the other nine sources don't already teach with a better evidence trail. For a B2B audience that tends to be more skeptical of urgency theater than a consumer one, the tactics-to-imitate-with-caution ratio here is worse than for the other nine.
